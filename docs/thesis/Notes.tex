\chapter{Introduction}
\section{Motivation}
Let us imagine that someone has to travel from IIT Madras to the airport.
The person has four realistic choices: car, bike, taxi, and bus. Based on
cost, comfort, and speed, the person may have a natural preference ranking
among these alternatives. For example, the ranking may be
\begin{equation}
    \text{Car} \succ \text{Taxi} \succ \text{Bike} \succ \text{Bus}.
\end{equation}
Now suppose that a fifth alternative is added. This alternative is walking,
which may take almost three hours and is therefore unlikely to be seriously
considered.
Common sense suggests that adding walking should not change the person's
relative preference between car, taxi, bike, and bus. Walking is an
irrelevant alternative in this situation. Therefore, the relative preference
between the original alternatives should remain unchanged.
This idea is called the \emph{Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives}
property. It is one of the central properties behind classical models of
choice.
However, human preferences may not always behave exactly as assumed by
classical multinomial logit models. Experimental evidence indicates that
adding or removing alternatives can change how attractive the existing
alternatives appear. Such behaviour is described as a violation of the
Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives property
\citep{seshadri2019}.

\section{Choice Data and Choice Sets}

Let \(\X\) denote the universal set of alternatives. A choice set is a
subset of \(\X\) containing the alternatives available to a decision-maker
at a particular time.

For the commute-mode example, the universal set is

\begin{equation}
    \X=
    \{
    \text{Car},
    \text{Taxi},
    \text{Bike},
    \text{Bus},
    \text{Walking}
    \}.
\end{equation}

The walking alternative remains a member of the universal set even when it
does not appear in a particular choice set. All the smaller choice sets are
constructed from the same fixed universal set.

Let \(A,B\subseteq\X\) be two choice sets. Let \(x\) and \(y\) be
alternatives belonging to the intersection \(A\cap B\).

\section{Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives}

For a universal set \(\X\) and choice sets \(A,B\subseteq\X\), the
Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives property for two alternatives
\(x\) and \(y\) can be written as

\begin{equation}
    \frac{P(x\mid A)}{P(y\mid A)}
    =
    \frac{P(x\mid B)}{P(y\mid B)},
    \qquad
    x,y\in A\cap B.
    \label{eq:iia}
\end{equation}

The equation states that if \(x\) and \(y\) are present in both choice sets,
then their relative odds should not change merely because other alternatives
have been added or removed.

\subsection{Worked Example}

For convenience, denote the alternatives as follows:

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Alternatives and their assigned weights}
    \label{tab:alternative-weights}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
        \toprule
        Item & Symbol & Weight \(w\)\\
        \midrule
        Car     & \(C\)  & \(0.40\)\\
        Taxi    & \(T\)  & \(0.25\)\\
        Bike    & \(Bi\) & \(0.15\)\\
        Bus     & \(Bu\) & \(0.12\)\\
        Walking & \(W\)  & \(0.08\)\\
        \midrule
        Total   &        & \(1.00\)\\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Consider the following two choice sets:

\begin{align}
    A &= \{C,T,Bi\},
    &
    \sum_{x\in A}w_x &= 0.80,
    \\
    B &= \{C,T,W\},
    &
    \sum_{x\in B}w_x &= 0.73.
\end{align}

We want to verify whether

\begin{equation}
    \frac{P(C\mid A)}{P(T\mid A)}
    =
    \frac{P(C\mid B)}{P(T\mid B)}.
\end{equation}

For choice set \(A\),

\begin{align}
    P(C\mid A)
    &=
    \frac{w_C}{\sum_{x\in A}w_x}
    =
    \frac{0.40}{0.80}
    =
    0.50,
    \\
    P(T\mid A)
    &=
    \frac{w_T}{\sum_{x\in A}w_x}
    =
    \frac{0.25}{0.80}
    =
    0.3125.
\end{align}

Therefore,

\begin{equation}
    \frac{P(C\mid A)}{P(T\mid A)}
    =
    \frac{0.40/0.80}{0.25/0.80}
    =
    \frac{0.40}{0.25}
    =
    1.6.
\end{equation}

For choice set \(B\),

\begin{align}
    P(C\mid B)
    &=
    \frac{w_C}{\sum_{x\in B}w_x}
    =
    \frac{0.40}{0.73},
    \\
    P(T\mid B)
    &=
    \frac{w_T}{\sum_{x\in B}w_x}
    =
    \frac{0.25}{0.73}.
\end{align}

Therefore,

\begin{equation}
    \frac{P(C\mid B)}{P(T\mid B)}
    =
    \frac{0.40/0.73}{0.25/0.73}
    =
    \frac{0.40}{0.25}
    =
    1.6.
\end{equation}

Thus,

\begin{equation}
    \frac{P(C\mid A)}{P(T\mid A)}
    =
    \frac{P(C\mid B)}{P(T\mid B)}.
\end{equation}

Hence, the Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives property holds in this
worked example.

\section{Multinomial Logit Model}

The classical multinomial logit model assigns a score \(v(x)\) to every
alternative \(x\). The probability of selecting \(x\) from choice set \(C\)
is

\begin{equation}
    P(x\mid C)
    =
    \frac{\exp(v(x))}
    {\displaystyle\sum_{y\in C}\exp(v(y))}.
    \label{eq:mnl}
\end{equation}

The scores are shift-invariant. A common identification constraint is

\begin{equation}
    \sum_{x\in\X}v(x)=0.
    \label{eq:mnl-identification}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Reason for the Identification Constraint}

Consider two alternatives, car and taxi, with scores

\begin{align}
    v(C)&=2.5,\\
    v(T)&=-0.9.
\end{align}

Their exponentiated scores are

\begin{align}
    \exp(v(C))
    &=
    \exp(2.5)
    \approx
    12.182,
    \\
    \exp(v(T))
    &=
    \exp(-0.9)
    \approx
    0.407.
\end{align}

Therefore, the probability of choosing car from the set
\(\{C,T\}\) is

\begin{align}
    P(C\mid\{C,T\})
    &=
    \frac{\exp(2.5)}
    {\exp(2.5)+\exp(-0.9)}
    \\
    &=
    \frac{12.182}{12.182+0.407}
    \\
    &\approx
    0.968.
\end{align}

However,

\begin{equation}
    v(C)+v(T)=2.5-0.9=1.6\neq 0.
\end{equation}

Suppose the same constant \(k\) is added to both scores. To satisfy the
zero-sum constraint, choose \(k\) such that

\begin{align}
    (2.5+k)+(-0.9+k)&=0,\\
    1.6+2k&=0,\\
    k&=-0.8.
\end{align}

After applying this shift,

\begin{align}
    v(C)&=2.5-0.8=1.7,\\
    v(T)&=-0.9-0.8=-1.7.
\end{align}

The probability obtained using these centred scores is

\begin{align}
    P(C\mid\{C,T\})
    &=
    \frac{\exp(1.7)}
    {\exp(1.7)+\exp(-1.7)}
    \\
    &=
    \frac{5.474}{5.474+0.183}
    \\
    &\approx
    0.968.
\end{align}

Thus, adding the same constant to every score does not change the choice
probabilities. The zero-sum constraint selects one representative from all
score vectors that produce the same probabilities.

\section{Context-Dependent Utility}

In a universal logit model, the utility of an alternative can depend on the
choice set in which it appears. Instead of assigning only one utility to an
alternative, the model allows the alternative to have different utilities
in different contexts.

The probability of selecting \(x\) from \(C\) is

\begin{equation}
    P(x\mid C)
    =
    \frac{\exp(u(x\mid C))}
    {\displaystyle\sum_{y\in C}\exp(u(y\mid C))}.
    \label{eq:context-logit}
\end{equation}

An identification constraint can be imposed separately for each choice set:

\begin{equation}
    \sum_{y\in C}u(y\mid C)=0,
    \qquad
    \text{for every }C\subseteq\X.
\end{equation}

\section{Decomposition of Context Effects}

\begin{lemma}[Decomposition of context-dependent utility]
For a universal logit model, the context-dependent utility \(u(x\mid C)\)
can be written as a sum of terms indexed by subsets \(B\) of
\(C\setminus\{x\}\) \citep{seshadri2019}:

\begin{equation}
    u(x\mid C)
    =
    \sum_{B\subseteq C\setminus\{x\}}v(x\mid B).
    \label{eq:utility-decomposition}
\end{equation}

Equivalently,

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    u(x\mid C)
    &=
    v(x)
    +
    \sum_{y\in C\setminus\{x\}}v(x\mid\{y\})
    \\
    &\quad+
    \sum_{\{y,z\}\subseteq C\setminus\{x\}}
    v(x\mid\{y,z\})
    +\cdots.
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The parameters satisfy

\begin{equation}
    \sum_{x\notin B}v(x\mid B)=0
    \qquad
    \text{for every }B\subseteq\X.
    \label{eq:decomposition-constraint}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}


\subsection{Worked Example}

Consider the choice set

\begin{equation}
    A=\{C,T,Bi\}.
\end{equation}

The subsets of \(A\) are

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \emptyset,\;
    \{C\},\;
    \{T\},\;
    \{Bi\},\;
    \{C,T\},\;
    \{T,Bi\},\;
    \{Bi,C\},\;
    \{C,T,Bi\}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The utility expansions are

\begin{align}
    u(C\mid A)
    &=
    v(C)
    +v(C\mid\{T\})
    +v(C\mid\{Bi\})
    +v(C\mid\{T,Bi\}),
    \\
    u(T\mid A)
    &=
    v(T)
    +v(T\mid\{C\})
    +v(T\mid\{Bi\})
    +v(T\mid\{C,Bi\}),
    \\
    u(Bi\mid A)
    &=
    v(Bi)
    +v(Bi\mid\{C\})
    +v(Bi\mid\{T\})
    +v(Bi\mid\{C,T\}).
\end{align}

The corresponding constraint equations are

\begin{align}
    v(C)+v(T)+v(Bi)&=0,
    \label{eq:constraint-empty}
    \\
    v(T\mid\{C\})+v(Bi\mid\{C\})&=0,
    \\
    v(C\mid\{T\})+v(Bi\mid\{T\})&=0,
    \\
    v(C\mid\{Bi\})+v(T\mid\{Bi\})&=0,
    \\
    v(C\mid\{T,Bi\})&=0,
    \\
    v(T\mid\{C,Bi\})&=0,
    \\
    v(Bi\mid\{C,T\})&=0.
\end{align}

The final three constraints force the corresponding highest-order terms
to zero. The utility equations therefore reduce to

\begin{align}
    u(C\mid A)
    &=
    v(C)
    +v(C\mid\{T\})
    +v(C\mid\{Bi\}),
    \\
    u(T\mid A)
    &=
    v(T)
    +v(T\mid\{C\})
    +v(T\mid\{Bi\}),
    \\
    u(Bi\mid A)
    &=
    v(Bi)
    +v(Bi\mid\{C\})
    +v(Bi\mid\{T\}).
\end{align}

There are nine remaining parameters. The first four constraint equations
give four independent restrictions. Therefore, the number of free parameters
is

\begin{equation}
    9-4=5.
\end{equation}

\chapter{Bradley--Terry Model and Maximum Likelihood}

To understand the underlying mechanics of a model for choice data, we
consider observed pairwise comparisons and assume a probabilistic model.
The Bradley--Terry model is used for this purpose.

The goal is to estimate unknown parameters that provide a compressed
representation of the observed comparison data.


For items \(i\) and \(j\), with scores \(\theta_i\) and \(\theta_j\), the
Bradley--Terry model defines

\begin{equation}
    P(i\succ j)
    =
    \frac{\exp(\theta_i)}
    {\exp(\theta_i)+\exp(\theta_j)}.
    \label{eq:bradley-terry}
\end{equation}

Dividing the numerator and denominator by \(\exp(\theta_i)\) gives

\begin{equation}
    P(i\succ j)
    =
    \frac{1}
    {1+\exp\left(-(\theta_i-\theta_j)\right)}.
\end{equation}

Therefore, the probability depends only on the score difference
\(\theta_i-\theta_j\).

\section{Likelihood and Log-Likelihood}

\subsection{Assumptions Used for Estimation}

The assumptions typically used when working with repeated Bernoulli
observations include independence and identical distribution.

\paragraph{Independence.}

For \(i\neq j\),

\begin{equation}
    P(X_i=x_i,X_j=x_j)
    =
    P(X_i=x_i)P(X_j=x_j).
\end{equation}

Equivalently, when the required conditional probabilities are defined,

\begin{equation}
    P(X_i=x_i\mid X_j=x_j)
    =
    P(X_i=x_i).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Identically distributed.}

For all \(i\) and \(j\),

\begin{equation}
    P(X_i=1)
    =
    P(X_j=1)
    =
    p.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Likelihood Function}

To obtain a principled estimate from observed data, we use a likelihood
function. The likelihood indicates which value of the unknown parameter
makes the observed data most probable.

Following Fisher's principle of maximum likelihood
\citep{fisher1922}, suppose that

\begin{equation}
    x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n
\end{equation}

are observations drawn from a distribution parameterised by \(p\).
The likelihood is the joint probability of the observed data, viewed as
a function of \(p\):

\begin{align}
    L(p;\{x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n\})
    &=
    P(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n;p)
    \\
    &=
    P(x_1;p)P(x_2;p)\cdots P(x_n;p)
    \qquad\text{(by independence)}
    \\
    &=
    \prod_{i=1}^{n}
    p^{x_i}(1-p)^{1-x_i}.
    \label{eq:bernoulli-likelihood}
\end{align}

The last line follows by treating each \(x_i\) as a Bernoulli observation
with success probability \(p\), so that

\begin{equation}
    P(x_i;p)
    =
    p^{x_i}(1-p)^{1-x_i}.
\end{equation}

The maximum likelihood estimate \(\widehat{p}\) is the value of \(p\)
that maximises the likelihood in \cref{eq:bernoulli-likelihood}.
